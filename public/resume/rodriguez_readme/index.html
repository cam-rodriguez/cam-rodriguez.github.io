Some work of mine that I’m particularly proud of includes:

I combed through physical and digital archives, created databases to keep track of names, places and pages, read thousands of pages of primary and secondary source materials and interviewed past and present administration, staff and experts.

Ultimately, I uncovered a narrative much different than what the university portrayed of itself, bringing to light a tradition of school-sponsored minstrel performances brought to the school by the clergy in charge, and proposed barriers to admission for Black students set by the university administration.

The story, in addition to ongoing research from other campus groups, led to change: the university renamed multiple spaces on campus, held a formal reconciliation ceremony for their institutional connection to slavery, and the library modified existing finding aids and materials to demarcate racist material.

Part of a larger project , this series broke down the use and abuse of restraint chairs in Illinois county jails. The chairs, which the U.N. deemed torture, are typically used for people who are detained and resisting orders in some form. Running off a tip and hoardes of incident report forms from the state, a team of reporters at Illinois Answers worked to create the first audit and accounting of how these chairs are used. We found that many people were restrained upward of 10+ hours at a time and that many were also handling withdrawal or a mental health crisis.

My role in the project included developing pipelines for data and document analysis and processing, including tinkering with different OCR methods and tools. After helping to create the initial database structure, reporter Grace Hauck took the lead in compiling and rigorously reporting out thousands of cases of restraints from 2019 to 2023. Alongside Hauck and a team of other reporters, interns and fellows, we fact-checked the database, comparing pages and pages of scanned reports with the entries. And once we had cleaned, fact-checked data, I created visualizations and interactives for the series, including a drop-down tool for people to view information county-by-county.

I also played a role in the second installment in the series, which focused on Coles County, Illinois. After analyzing some data, the team noticed a pattern of “stun cuffs” being used in tandem with the chairs. The cuffs generate a log entry every time they’re deployed, and I was able to triage, process and visualize the data that came from them to strengthen the reporting done by Hauck.

Part historical deep-dive and part enterprise feature, I met with many residents, activists and organizers on and off the record to talk about their concerns with a proposed shipping and logistics development and the idea of what real community engagement would look like for them.

Like the other projects I had done within the Illinois Answers newsroom, this was a collaboration from the start.

My co-reporters, Alex Nitkin of Illinois Answers and A.D. Quig of Chicago Tribune, and I worked to chase down a tip from a former Cook County township assessor, who said that the county was routinely and deliberately misclassifying properties as vacant, including missing entire streets and subdivisions. Instead, the parcels were home sometimes to multi-million dollar mansions, with the owners paying a vacant land tax rate.

With support from Alex and A.D., I reverse engineered the county assessment system to find hundreds of parcels around the county that had been classed vacant despite having homes or businesses built on them, or having substantial additions to their properties. Bolstered by the on-the-ground reporting of my partners, we scrubbed through satellite imagery, parcel sale information, deeds, permit data and internal assessment systems, and found over $400 million in missing revenue from approximately 620 properties that had been misclassified or missed altogehter.

Since the story released, the county has sped up its overhaul of its field inspection system, and more taxpayers have been kept informed about the system. The county also has audited its own system, based on our reporting and analysis, and added nearly half a billion in tax revenue back on the rolls by correcting the errors we identified.

“Find out what happens to your school under the IPS Rebuilding Stronger Plan”, “IPS wants Rebuilding Stronger to address inequities, but some worry its approach is unfair”, published in Chalkbeat Indiana throughout 2022, and “Did PAC money scare away school board candidates in Indianapolis?", published in Chalkbeat Indiana in collaboration with WFYI/Indianapolis NPR

When I was a data and graphics reporter at Chalkbeat, a large part of my role was collaborating with local bureaus across the country, often working on hyperlocal stories. I can’t think of a better example of working closely with a reporter on the ground like the reporting I did alongside Amelia Pak-Harvey on Indianapolis Public Schools and Marion County.

When IPS launched a program called Rebuilding Stronger, parents were deeply confused about what change was going to come to their kids’ schools — especially because the program changed seemingly every week. With Amelia’s help, updates and meeting notes, we created interactives that we embedded into each story in the series, like maps, searchable tables, and additional charts and graphs for context. We got direct feedback from parents about the impact that the tools had for them and how helpful they were to understand closures, upgrades and consolidations.

Pak-Harvey and I continued our collaboration with another story on school board funding in the 2022 election in Marion County. She had noticed a major uptick in donations to pro-charter board candidates from a patchwork of PACs, and was curious about the connections between fundraising and actually winning. From there, I went through and digitized hundreds of pages of election results, and Pak-Harvey and a reporter at WFYI compiled campaign finance data. We pulled things together into a few charts and a larger feature about the connections, proving her hunch and highlighting the interconnectedness for readers.

Without a doubt, this story highlighted just how important backgrounding and fact-checking is in the reporting process. Alongside Jewél Jackson, this story was born out of looking for solutions to Chicago’s pervasive lead-contaminated water.

With both of us having experience on the ed beat, we were familiar with the problems that Chicago Public Schools had with lead, that they opened up about in 2016 following reporting from the Chicago Tribune and Better Government Association. But lead in water in Chicago isn’t a new story. Or was it?

Chicago Public Schools, with over 550 buildings, often boasts about its lead testing program being top of the line, state of the art, and catching lead as it comes up in different buildings. After the district coordinated a live demo of its testing program, they also highlighted a specific new invention as a solution for the district’s water woes, invented by a local school engineer: the Noah. We did more reporting, were prepared to file a draft on the device, until we decided to fact check the patent status of the device.

On the last page of the document was the engineer’s name… right next to the name of the head of the district’s lead abatement program. Then the snowball kept growing: the company was incorporated at an address of the district official, who had also used a lawyer he’d used for his previous businesses. And to top it off, the official was actively promoting the device to other school districts on CPS time, while the engineer was test-running the device in district schools. At no point did they disclose knowing each other outside of distant collagues.

Our investigation highlighted the violations of the district’s board of ethics, the close relationship and the concerns it raises with the efficacy of the district’s lead abatement program, of which it had sunk millions into testing and virtually none into developing a fix.
